In military doctrine, _jointness_ refers to the integration and coordination of different branches of a single nation’s armed forces—typically the Army, Navy, Air Force, and other service branches—into unified operations, planning, and command structures. It is distinct from “combined” operations, which involve the armed forces of multiple nations.

## Origins & Evolution

The term gained formal use during the Second World War to distinguish national inter-service cooperation (“joint”) from multinational cooperation (“combined”). This distinction was formalized in the [[Arcadia Conference]] (Dec 1941–Jan 1942), where Anglo-American planners agreed:
    
    > “The word _Joint_ [should] be applied to inter-service collaboration of one nation and the word _Combined_ to collaboration between two or more of the United Nations.”
    
## Historical Context

Before WWII, most militaries operated with siloed service branches. The U.S. and U.K. developed more cohesive joint staff structures during the war (e.g., U.S. Joint Chiefs of Staff, British Chiefs of Staff Committee). _Jointness_ helped solve issues of coordination in theaters like the Pacific and Europe where air, sea, and ground forces had to operate together.

## Modern Use & Significance

- NATO and most Western militaries have adopted joint doctrines and training.
- The U.S. codified jointness in the Goldwater-Nichols Act of 1986, mandating joint command structures and career paths for officers.
- Modern "joint operations" are now standard, especially in complex expeditionary environments.
- While _joint_ operations pertain to intra-national coordination, they are the building blocks for effective _combined_ (multinational) operations.
- Effective jointness enhances interoperability with other nations by streamlining logistics, command, and communications internally.
